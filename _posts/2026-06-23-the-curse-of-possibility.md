One thing I’ve been thinking about lately:

Life today might actually be psychologically harder than it was for most of history.

Not physically harder.

If you were a peasant in medieval Europe, life was brutal. You worked harder than I ever will. You were poorer. You died younger.

But there was one thing you didn’t have to figure out.

What to do with your life.

If your father farmed potatoes, you farmed potatoes.

If your father was a blacksmith, you became a blacksmith.

If you were born a noble, you were a noble.

Most people never had to answer the question:

“What do I want to be?”

The answer had already been decided.

Today it’s the opposite.

We ask kids that question from the time they’re five years old.

“What do you want to be when you grow up?”

The older I get, the crazier that question sounds.

How is anybody supposed to know?

You can become a doctor.

A lawyer.

A software engineer.

A founder.

A salesperson.

A content creator.

A soldier.

A teacher.

And that’s before you even start thinking about where to live, who to marry, whether to have kids, or what kind of life you want.

Every choice closes off a thousand others.

That’s a heavy burden.

I think that’s why so many people feel stuck.

Not because they have too few options.

Because they have too many.

A medieval farmer probably wasn’t lying awake wondering whether he should have started a software company.

The option didn’t exist.

He may have had less freedom.

But he also had less uncertainty.

Today we’re freer than any generation in history.

But we’re also responsible for designing our own lives.

And I’m not sure humans evolved for that.

Sometimes I wonder if a lot of modern anxiety comes from this simple fact:

Our ancestors suffered because they had too few choices.

We suffer because we have too many.

Carlos
